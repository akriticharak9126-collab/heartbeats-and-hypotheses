# We Said We Were Saving Them

I visited Manda Zoo and saw a bear growling, confined to the space it had been given. I can't say what that growl meant, and as you'll see, the science of reading an animal's state is less simple than it looks. But I remember standing there and feeling empathy for him. He was away from his home in the jungle, and I thought of what I had read in school: that wild animals are supposed to be in the wild, and free. I didn't yet have the words for what was bothering me. I have a few more of them now, which is partly why I'm writing this.

Let's start with a slightly awkward thought.

Zoos count as a method of ex-situ conservation, which is a fancy way of saying "protecting wildlife away from where it lives." Say that out loud and it sounds odd, doesn't it? We take wild animals out of their forests, grasslands and oceans, and keep them in spaces far from everything they know: their home, their community, their freedom. I find it hard to hear that as protection.

Part of what bothers me is how easily the protection can start to serve us. We build the enclosure, invite the crowds, sell the tickets, and somewhere along the way a living animal becomes an attraction, occasionally even a tourist destination. I'd like to know when that happened, because their existence stopped being only theirs at some point and became our entertainment.

There's a quieter cost, too. Animals in captivity can lose the chance to do the things that make them who they are in the wild: hunting, foraging, exploring, migrating, finding their way through a landscape they know by heart. It's as if, in our effort to preserve their existence, we risk trimming away the very things that make that existence natural.

Try putting yourself in their place. You're pulled away from your home, your people and everything familiar, and slowly you start depending on someone else just to get by. Would a bowl of food and a roof be enough to convince you that you were free? It's an imperfect comparison, since I can't know what a tiger experiences, and part of what follows is about how scientists try to find out anyway.

Maybe that's the biggest contradiction in how we practise conservation: wildlife was never ours to begin with, and in trying to save it we sometimes forget that. Conservation should serve the animals, not our curiosity or our weekend plans.

So how is this conservation? I don't think I can answer that until I'm clear about what the word is supposed to mean, and for that I need to look at what a life behind walls actually involves.

# But What Does a Life Behind Walls Look Like?

To understand what captivity does, we first have to understand what a habitat does for an animal.

A tiger doesn't just live in a forest. She works it: patrolling a territory, hunting, marking her surroundings, reading the presence of everyone else who shares the space. An elephant doesn't just stand among other elephants. She builds relationships, communicates, remembers, and travels across the land in search of food and water. For these animals, home isn't a backdrop. It's the stage where their biology and behaviour actually get to happen.

Once they enter captivity, that stage changes dramatically. The food arrives on schedule, health is monitored, physical needs are looked after. But the chance to perform certain natural behaviours is, sadly, restricted.

That raises a question I don't think gets enough attention: can an animal be physically healthy and still have poor welfare?

#### When behaviour becomes a warning sign

Picture an animal walking the same stretch of its enclosure, turn after turn, as if tracing a groove only it can see. Scientists call this kind of repetitive behaviour with no obvious immediate purpose stereotypy. Pacing, swaying and following the same route again and again are examples seen in some captive animals.

It's one of the most discussed indicators of compromised welfare, and in their 2004 paper [Can't Stop, Won't Stop: Is Stereotypy a Reliable Animal Welfare Indicator?](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/animal-welfare/article/cant-stop-wont-stop-is-stereotypy-a-reliable-animal-welfare-indicator/FC0A792BF04EFAC4464543453A0D2155), Georgia Mason and Nora Latham looked at exactly how reliable it is. They found that environments which induce or increase stereotypies are frequently linked with poorer welfare. But the picture isn't tidy. Animals may keep up these behaviours even after their circumstances improve, and the behaviour itself may work as a coping mechanism.

So a stereotypy isn't a verdict. It's more like a flag that says look closer: a warning sign that calls for investigation, not an automatic diagnosis of suffering.

[Clubb and Mason](http://hdl.handle.net/10214/4732) (2007) added another piece by examining how a species' natural behavioural biology relates to carnivore welfare. They found that species which naturally range over wide areas can be especially hard to keep successfully in captivity. Polar bears were among those showing significant difficulties, both with stereotypic behaviour and with breeding.

The uncomfortable implication is that a space that looks generous to a human visitor can still fall short when measured against what the species actually needs, behaviourally and ecologically.

#### Survival is not the whole story

Welfare isn't settled by food, water and veterinary care alone. It also depends on behavioural opportunities, social relationships, environmental stimulation, and an animal's overall physical and psychological state. An animal can survive in captivity, but survival on its own can't tell us whether those needs are being met.

This is where I think our definition of conservation has been too narrow. We've become good at keeping many wild animals alive outside their natural habitats. I'm less sure we've always learned to give them lives worth living.

# Somewhere Along the Way, they Became an Attraction

There's another side to captivity we can't skip: the way wildlife and human entertainment have become tangled together.

For plenty of visitors, a zoo is the one place they'll ever meet an animal they've only seen in books or on screens. Children stare up at elephants. Families line up to photograph giraffes. I know there's real educational potential in that moment, and I don't want to wave it away.

But somewhere between observing wildlife and turning it into an attraction, the line can quietly blur. Are we helping people understand animals, or are we simply making animals available for people to enjoy?

#### The economics of exhibition

Zoos and wildlife attractions don't run on good intentions alone. Ticket sales, tourism, merchandise, animal encounters and other visitor experiences can all help keep them financially afloat. That isn't inherently wrong. Conservation needs funding, trained professionals, veterinary care and long-term institutional support.

The trouble starts when what visitors want begins to outrank what animals need. An animal shouldn't have to perform, greet the public, or stay constantly on view just because someone paid for a ticket. I don't think anyone objects to people appreciating wildlife. What I'd want to know is whether that appreciation costs the animal being appreciated something.

#### Can a zoo actually change how we think?

It would be unfair to say zoos offer no educational or conservation value, so let's look at the evidence.

A 2025 [meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1111/cobi.14237) by McNally and colleagues, published in Conservation Biology, pulled together 56 studies on how zoo and aquarium engagement affects visitors. The researchers found a small to medium positive overall effect on conservation-related knowledge, beliefs and reported behaviour. Visitors tended to know more, hold more favourable attitudes, and say they were more likely to take actions that could help biodiversity.

That matters. It suggests zoos can be genuinely meaningful spaces for conservation education.

Still, there's a gap between learning about biodiversity and actively protecting it, and what visitors report they'll do isn't the same as what they actually do afterwards. Good attitudes don't automatically turn into lasting change. For me, the true test of zoo education isn't how many people walk through the gates, but how many leave with a deeper sense of responsibility toward the natural world, and whether that sense of responsibility lasts.

So if captivity has this many limits, why do zoos still have a place in conservation? Because for some species the alternative isn't a better life in the wild. It's no life at all, and that was the part I couldn't responsibly leave out.

# And Yet, It's Not That Simple

It's easy to question captivity when we focus on the freedom an animal loses. It gets a lot messier when we think about the animals that might not survive at all without us.

Imagine a species whose habitat has been destroyed, whose numbers have crashed, and whose last individuals face growing threats. Would keeping a carefully managed population outside its natural home really be wrong?

Not if it gives that species a genuine chance to avoid extinction. That's where ex-situ conservation earns its place.

#### When captivity becomes a chance at survival

Captive breeding programmes can play a significant role in protecting threatened species. Through careful management, conservationists can keep populations going outside their natural habitats, look after genetic diversity, and, in the right circumstances, produce individuals for future reintroduction.

Genetic diversity deserves special attention. A population with limited genetic variation can face higher risks from inbreeding and a reduced ability to adapt to environmental change. Scientific population management can help tackle some of those problems.

But more animals doesn't automatically mean a successful programme. Genetic health, population sustainability and the availability of suitable natural habitat all have to be part of the picture.

#### Rescue, rehabilitation and second chances

Not every animal in captivity is there to be looked at. Some have been rescued from the illegal wildlife trade. Some were injured by human activity, orphaned, or pushed out of their habitats. For them, rehabilitation can be a chance to recover and, where possible, return to the wild.

Think of an elephant rescued from exploitation, or an injured bird that can no longer fly. Their immediate needs may call for human care, veterinary treatment and a protected space. In moments like these, intervention isn't necessarily an act of taking freedom away. It can be an attempt to restore the possibility of living freely.

But rescue and permanent exhibition are not the same thing. Wherever it's biologically and practically possible, the goal should be recovery and a return to an appropriate natural environment.

#### Returning to where they belong

Reintroduction programmes aim to return organisms to places within their former natural range where they've disappeared or become locally extinct.

But releasing animals isn't as simple as opening a gate. The habitat has to be suitable, the original threats addressed, disease risks assessed, and the released population monitored. The IUCN's [Guidelines for Reintroductions and Other Conservation Translocations](https://portals.iucn.org/library/efiles/documents/2013-009.pdf) (2013) stress that conservation translocations need careful planning, scientific justification and long-term evaluation. A released animal can't be expected to thrive in a habitat that is still destroyed or unsafe.

That's the limit of what captivity can do: it can help preserve a species, but on its own it can't repair the ecological conditions that caused the decline in the first place. So the purpose of ex-situ conservation shouldn't be to make captivity permanent. It should be to help build a future where wildlife can survive beyond it, wherever possible.

# Can a Cage Really Be a Second Chance?

I don't think the answer is to throw out every zoo, and I don't think it's to defend every form of captivity either. What's left is a harder job: deciding what we should expect conservation institutions to deliver.

If we accept that captivity can create welfare problems and that it can protect threatened species, the next question is a practical one: how do we make conservation more ethical?

#### Giving animals more than an enclosure

An enclosure shouldn't exist just to contain an animal. It should give her room to be the animal she is. That's where environmental enrichment comes in: changing an animal's surroundings to encourage appropriate behaviour and improve welfare. Depending on the species, it might mean:

• Chances to forage and search for food

• Structures that invite exploration

• Opportunities to climb, swim or dig

• A suitable social environment

• Varied sensory experiences

• More choice and control over their surroundings

[Mason, Clubb, Latham and Vickery](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.applanim.2006.05.041) (2007) looked at how enrichment can address stereotypic behaviour. Their key insight is that enrichment should tackle the motivations underneath repetitive behaviours, rather than just trying to stop the movements we can see.

But enrichment isn't magic. How well it works varies, and some well-established abnormal behaviours can persist even after the environment improves. That's why it has to go hand in hand with continuous welfare assessment.

#### Conservation should be species-specific

A tiger, an elephant and a primate can't be expected to thrive under the same conditions. Their movement patterns, social lives, feeding habits and ecological needs are all different. So enclosure design and animal care should be built around what each species biologically needs, not around what looks pleasant to a human visitor.

The people doing the caring matter just as much. In their paper [Zoo Animal Welfare: The Human Dimension](https://doi.org/10.1080/10888705.2018.1513839) (2018), Cole and Fraser highlighted how the actions, knowledge, consistency and attitudes of animal-care staff can shape welfare outcomes. Welfare isn't only a design problem. It also depends on the people who understand and look after the individuals living inside.

#### Taking conservation beyond the zoo

One of the most important solutions lies outside the zoo altogether: strengthening conservation where wildlife naturally belongs. Habitat protection, restoring degraded ecosystems, wildlife corridors, anti-poaching initiatives and community-based conservation all help protect species in their own environments.

These approaches tackle something captive breeding alone can't: the loss of the ecosystems wildlife depends on. A truly successful programme shouldn't only ask how many animals we can keep in captivity. It should also ask whether the natural world is becoming safer for them to return to.

Perhaps this is where our thinking needs to shift most. Instead of treating captivity as the centre of conservation, we could see it as one tool among many in a much larger effort to protect biodiversity.

# Maybe We Have Been Asking the Wrong Question

I started with a pointed question: how can we call something conservation if it takes animals away from the very places they evolved to live? After looking at the science, my honest answer is that it depends. It isn't as simple as "all zoos are wrong" or "all captivity is necessary." Some captive environments create serious welfare problems. Some institutions contribute meaningfully to threatened species management, rescue, research and education. Some animals may need permanent human care, while others may have a realistic shot at returning to the wild.

The difference comes down to purpose, practice, accountability and outcomes.

I think we've been looking at conservation through an incomplete lens. We've measured success in population numbers, breeding records and survival rates. But those numbers can't, on their own, tell us what life is like for an individual animal, or how healthy the ecosystem around it really is.

A species isn't truly secured just because individuals are preserved while the habitats, ecological relationships and natural processes it depends on keep disappearing. At the same time, refusing every form of human intervention would ignore a hard reality: human activity has already pushed many species into situations where carefully managed help may be necessary.

So the question is no longer just should zoos exist? When I look at any institution that calls itself a conservation centre, this is what I'd want to know:

• Do its animals have the chance to express natural behaviours?

• Does it contribute to measurable conservation outcomes?

• Does it support habitat protection?

• Does it put welfare ahead of entertainment?

• Does it recognise that the animals in its care are individuals with needs that stretch far beyond our wish to look at them?

We can't undo every consequence of human activity, but we can rethink how we respond to it. Conservation shouldn't be about owning wildlife, displaying it, or simply keeping it alive so our generation can watch. It should be about protecting the conditions in which wildlife can keep existing, evolving and thriving without depending entirely on us.

The greatest achievement of conservation wouldn't be a world where every endangered animal has a place behind an enclosure. It would be a world where fewer animals need to be kept there in the first place.
